Introduction to databases

1. What is a database?

A database is an organized collection of data stored so that people and programs can find, add, change, and remove information quickly. Think of a school's filing room, but searchable in a fraction of a second.

Most databases are built from tables. The student table above has three parts you will see throughout this site:

TableA group of related data about one kind of thing, such as students.
Record (row)One complete entry, such as everything about Mei Tanaka.
Field (column)One type of fact, such as Major or GPA.

2. Why are databases important?

Without a database, information ends up scattered across papers, files, and inboxes. Databases solve that:

Fast searchingFind one student among a million in moments.
Accurate dataRules stop bad entries, such as a GPA of 9.5 or a duplicate ID.
Shared accessMany people use the same up-to-date data at once.
Security and backupOnly approved users see data, and copies protect against loss.

3. What is a Database Management System (DBMS)?

A DBMS is the software that sits between people and the stored data. It creates the database, controls who can use it, answers questions (queries), and keeps the data safe and consistent. Examples include MySQL, PostgreSQL, Oracle, Microsoft SQL Server, and SQLite.

Users never touch the files directly. They ask the DBMS, and it does the work.

4. How are databases different from spreadsheets?

Both store rows and columns, so they look alike. They behave very differently once the data grows or many people need it.

SpreadsheetDatabase
Best forCalculations, charts, small listsLarge, connected data that changes often
SizeSlows down with hundreds of thousands of rowsHandles millions of records
Many usersEdits can clash or overwrite each otherMany people edit safely at the same time
Data rulesAny value can go in any cellTypes and rules reject invalid data
RelationshipsData is often repeated across sheetsTables link through keys, so data is stored once
SearchingFilters and lookupsPowerful queries written in SQL
SecurityWhole-file accessPermissions per user, table, or field

Example: a spreadsheet can list students. A database can also connect each student to their courses, grades, and bills without copying their name everywhere.
